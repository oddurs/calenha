# decisions

A running log. Newest at the bottom. Each entry: what was decided, and why.

### D-001 · the language is data; the code is a compiler
All language content lives in TOML under `lang/`. The Python toolkit reads it,
validates it and renders it. Like a design system: tokens are the source of truth;
everything else is generated.

### D-002 · python, zero runtime dependencies
Python 3.11+ for `tomllib` in the standard library. Quick to change while the
language is still finding itself. Dev tools (pytest, ruff) via `uv`.

### D-003 · stress rests on the second-to-last syllable
The Portuguese music. Two exceptions, both audible in the spelling:
a word ending in a nasal (*mirão*, *nevoã*) draws stress to its end, and an acute
accent marks a long vowel, which always takes the stress (*thalírë*).

### D-004 · c is always /k/
As in Quenya. There is no soft *c* and no letter *k* in the modern spelling; *k*
survives only in old forms.

### D-005 · final e is written ë
Borrowed from Tolkien. It reminds the reader the vowel is sounded, and it gives
word-endings a little lift on the page. A plain *e* may not end a word, and *ë* may
appear nowhere else.

### D-006 · digraphs ch, lh, nh, th
*ch* /ʃ/, *lh* /ʎ/, *nh* /ɲ/ from Portuguese; *th* /θ/ from English. *lh* and *nh*
never begin a word, as in Portuguese, which keeps them soft and medial.

### D-007 · no imperative mood
The plain verb is an invitation. See `context/principles.md` §6.

### D-008 · tense is a tide
Past and future are marked by the particles *vaza* (ebb) and *chea* (flood);
the present, slack water, is unmarked. From Portuguese *vazante* and *cheia*.

### D-009 · the breath mark ·
A middle dot. Between words it is a pause that closes the scope of a particle
(the language's quiet nod to a block ending). Inside a word it breaks the word open
with longing: *mi·rão*, "a memory that hurts to hold".

### D-010 · the land is Solmara, the people the Calenhi
*Solmara*, sun and sea. *Calenhi*, the calm ones, formed from *calenha* by the
people-suffix *-i*.

### D-011 · old Calenha, and the order of its changes
The language has a history: an older, harder speech (k, doubled vowels, plain
endings) worn smooth over generations. Changes run in order, as real sound change
does. The long vowels formed *before* n and l softened — otherwise *\*thaliire*
would have become *thalhirë*. Order is part of the story; `calenha check` holds every
old form to it.

### D-012 · taste is data
The generator's ear lives in `[euphony]` in `lang/phonology.toml`: tiers of sounds
(flow, breath, hush, spice) and how welcome each is. It rewards softness, open
syllables, varied vowels and an unhurried rhythm, and penalises stammers and
colliding vowels. It only proposes. A person reads the word aloud and chooses.

### D-013 · no noun may end in a u-glide
A word ending in *-au*, *-eu* or *-ou* looks like a verb's invitation. Nouns,
names and qualities avoid it, as they avoid a plain final *-u*.

### D-014 · the dictionary page (superseded in part by D-015)
One quiet column on fog-grey, set in Gentium Book Plus (made for linguists; it
carries IPA properly). The only colour that speaks is poppy gold, under each
stressed syllable — so the page teaches the language's music as it is read. The
opening line breathes in word by word, once.

### D-015 · the website (superseded by D-025)
The single dictionary page grew into a static site, generated by `calenha build` with
no dependencies: plain HTML, one stylesheet, links with explicit `.html` so it works
anywhere. Every word has its own page — how to say it, what it is made from, how it
changed from Old Calenha, its family, and where it is used. Calenha words in example
lines link to their pages. The look of D-014 carries through every page.

### D-016 · the unspoken
The site keeps a page of words the generator has found but no one has given a
meaning. They change as the lexicon grows (the seed is the size of the lexicon). It
is an invitation, in keeping with the language: here are sounds waiting for a sense.

### D-017 · counting stops at ten
Numbers one to ten, then **mil**: as many as the waves. Higher numbers are built by
adding (*desa una*, eleven) and are rarely needed. A people who count tides do not
count much else.

### D-018 · question words, and porcë
Questions are wonderings: *sera* + a question word. *Porcë* is both *why* and
*because*, from Portuguese *porquê*/*porque*.

### D-019 · no word for please, owning, or dying
Each absence is deliberate. Verbs already invite, so *please* is unneeded. *Tenu* is
to hold, never to own. The dead are *mirida*, the remembered; there is no verb for
dying. Do not fill these gaps.

### D-020 · new endings
*-eira* (one who does), *-ida* (one to whom it was done), *-al* (a place where it
gathers), *-osa* (full of; colours), and the first prefix, *vol-* (around again).

### D-021 · numbers are their own part of speech
A number precedes what it counts and the noun stays as it is.

### D-022 · texts
Longer pieces live in `lang/texts/`, validated like phrases. Their word-by-word
glosses are generated from the lexicon (each word's first sense), so the dictionary
stays the single source of meaning. The first three: *nevoã* (a poem), *lenda dë
Miraluma* (the creation story: forests on land are echoes of the kelp forests), and
*venida* (the blessing for a guest).

### D-023 · the reader
The site's reader glosses any line typed into it. It carries a small copy of the
lexicon (stress already marked, glosses already cut) rather than reimplementing the
phonology in the browser, so there is one set of rules, in Python.

### D-024 · word addresses
A word's page is its spelling without accents (`thalirë` → `thalire.html`). When two
words collide, a nasal is spelled out (`nã` → `nan.html`), and failing that the gloss
is added (`salva-thank-you.html`).

### D-025 · the website is SvelteKit
The site moved from a Python page generator to SvelteKit 2 with Svelte 5 and the
static adapter: every page is prerendered, so it is still plain files on GitHub
Pages. Python stays the compiler (`calenha export`); Svelte is the presenter. The
line reader in `web/src/lib/language/line.ts` only splits and looks up — stress
comes from the export, so there is still one set of rules.

### D-026 · the data contract
`calenha export` writes one JSON document with a `schema` number. Its shape is
mirrored by `web/src/lib/language/types.ts`, and the site refuses to build against a
schema it doesn't know. The export is generated, never committed.

### D-027 · the design system (superseded by D-029–D-033)
Tokens in three layers: primitives (named for Solmara: fog, kelp, tide, granite,
poppy), semantic roles (page, ink, accent, rule, stress), and component tokens.
Only the semantic layer changes between light and dark. Components use semantic and
component tokens only. The type scale is 1.2 on an 18px base, with fluid display
steps; one modular space scale; slow motion tokens. One family, Gentium Book Plus,
self-hosted. The system documents itself at `/design`, reading live token values.

### D-028 · pinned to known majors
SvelteKit 2, Vite 7, TypeScript 5, ESLint 9, Vitest 3 — the latest of each major
line rather than the newest majors (SvelteKit 3, Vite 8, TypeScript 7), which were
weeks old when the site was built. Move up deliberately, one major at a time.

### D-029 · the page keeps Calenha time
Light and dark are replaced by five hours: *alva* (dawn), *nevoã* (morning fog, the
default), *tardë* (afternoon), *solouro* (golden hour) and *noitë* (night). Each is a
full set of semantic tokens selected by `data-hour`, which may sit on any element,
so a part of a page can be shown at another hour. Following the sun, the hour comes
from the viewer's clock; a dark system preference always means night, and a light
preference is never plunged into night (the fog holds). Anyone can choose an hour
at the foot of a page. The hour is set before first paint by a script whose source
is the same `pickHour` function the tests cover. Every text/ground pair was checked:
ink on page is above 13:1 in every hour, muted text above 4.5:1.

### D-030 · two voices
Calenha is always set in Alegreya, a calligraphic serif; English in Alegreya Sans,
its humanist sibling. The rule is keyed to the `lang` attribute (`x-calenha`), so
the type itself says which language you are reading. The export tags emphasised
Calenha inside the documents for the same reason. IPA is set in Noto Serif:
Fontsource's Gentium subsets omit the IPA block, so the earlier site had been
falling back glyph by glyph for ɲ, ʎ, ɾ and friends.

### D-031 · the beat
Stress is marked by a short poppy bar spanning the stressed syllable, just above
its tallest letter: the one bright mark in the system, and it means only stress. It
shows wherever a word stands alone (headwords, titles, word pages, the reader,
word-by-word reading) and waits for a pointer in running lines, where marks would
crowd the writing. The earlier gold underline read as a link.

### D-032 · structure: sections, the margin, two names
Four sections (dictionary, language, reading, world), each with its pages as tabs.
Every page is laid out on a margin grid: a narrow column for labels, letters and
notes beside a column for reading, like a glossed manuscript or a field notebook.
Every page is named twice: in English in the margin, to find your way, and in
Calenha across the page, to read — and the Calenha title is a real word that links
to its own entry. Texts are set as a facing translation, the convention of a
bilingual edition.

### D-033 · the horizon
The home page opens on sky over sea in the hour's colours, with the sun, or the
moon, where the hour puts it: high in the fog, setting into the sea at golden hour.
Its one gradient is the light gathering at the horizon. It is the site's single
orchestrated moment, with the opening line arriving word by word.
